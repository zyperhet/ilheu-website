Three thousand metres beneath the surface of the Atlantic, where sunlight has never reached and the pressure would crush a human body like a paper cup, life thrives in conditions that would seem impossible anywhere else on Earth.

The hydrothermal vents of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — the submarine mountain range on which the Azores sit — are among the planet's most extraordinary ecosystems. Here, superheated water laden with minerals erupts from the ocean floor at temperatures exceeding 300°C, creating chimney-like structures of deposited minerals around which entire communities of organisms have evolved.

## An Alien World

The vent fields around the Azores — with names like Lucky Strike, Menez Gwen, and Rainbow — have been studied by marine scientists for over two decades. What they have found challenges fundamental assumptions about the requirements for life.

These ecosystems are powered not by sunlight and photosynthesis, as virtually all other life on Earth is, but by chemosynthesis — the harvesting of chemical energy from the minerals dissolved in vent fluids. Bacteria that can metabolise hydrogen sulphide form the base of a food chain that supports tube worms, blind shrimp, and mussels found nowhere else on the planet.

"When we first discovered these communities, some biologists didn't believe us," says Sofia Ribeiro, a marine biologist at the University of the Azores who has spent fifteen years studying vent ecosystems. "Life without sunlight? Life at 300 degrees? It seemed impossible. Now we understand that it's not only possible — it may be how life on Earth began."

## Why It Matters

The deep-sea vents of the Azores matter for reasons that extend far beyond academic curiosity.

First, they are a living laboratory for understanding the origins of life. If chemosynthesis can support complex ecosystems in the crushing darkness of the deep ocean, similar processes might support life on other worlds — beneath the ice of Jupiter's moon Europa, or in the subsurface oceans of Saturn's Enceladus.

Second, the mineral deposits around hydrothermal vents contain significant concentrations of copper, zinc, gold, and rare earth elements — materials essential to the green energy transition. Deep-sea mining companies have already expressed interest in exploiting these deposits, raising urgent questions about the balance between resource extraction and ecosystem preservation.

Third, the organisms that thrive in these extreme environments produce enzymes and compounds with potential applications in medicine, industry, and biotechnology. Several heat-stable enzymes derived from vent organisms are already used in PCR testing — the same technology that powered COVID-19 diagnostics worldwide.

## The Azores' Responsibility

As the landmass closest to these vent fields, the Azores occupy a unique position. The Regional Government has designated several vent sites as marine protected areas, but enforcement in waters three kilometres deep is inherently challenging.

"We are custodians of something extraordinary," Ribeiro says. "These ecosystems took millions of years to develop. A single mining operation could destroy them in weeks. The question is whether we have the wisdom to protect what we cannot easily see."

It is a question that echoes across the Azores' relationship with the Atlantic — a relationship that has always been defined by the tension between exploitation and stewardship, between taking from the sea and learning to leave it alone.

The vents will continue to erupt, the chimneys will continue to grow, and the blind shrimp will continue to dance in water hot enough to melt lead. Whether humans will let them is another matter entirely.
