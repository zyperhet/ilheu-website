The *vigias* — the stone watchtowers from which whalers once scanned the ocean for spouts — still stand on headlands across the Azores. Most are abandoned now, their whitewashed walls crumbling, their iron railings rusted by decades of salt wind. A few have been converted into whale-watching lookout points, their purpose inverted with a neatness that history rarely provides.

Until 1986, the Azores were the last place in the Atlantic where whales were hunted from open boats using hand-thrown harpoons. The industry had persisted here long after it had disappeared elsewhere, sustained by a combination of economic necessity, cultural tradition, and the sheer abundance of sperm whales that pass through the archipelago's waters.

## The Last Whalers

The men who remember the hunts are old now. In the harbour bars of Lajes do Pico — once the whaling capital of the Azores — they sit with coffee and aguardente and tell stories that their grandchildren find hard to believe.

Manuel Silveira, 84, was a harpooner for twenty years. He killed his first whale at nineteen. "It was not sport," he says, with the directness of someone who has been misunderstood too many times. "It was work. Hard, dangerous work. We did it because our families needed to eat."

The boats were open canoes, powered by oars and a small sail. The harpoons were thrown by hand. A single hunt could last hours, the whale dragging the boat across miles of open ocean before exhaustion and blood loss finally ended the contest.

"People today think of it as cruelty," Silveira says. "I understand that. But they did not live in these islands in the 1960s. There was no tourism. There were no airlines. There was the sea, and what the sea provided."

## Sanctuary

Today, the waters around the Azores are a marine sanctuary. Whale-watching has replaced whale-hunting, and the archipelago has become one of the world's premier destinations for cetacean tourism. Over twenty species of whale and dolphin have been recorded in Azorean waters, drawn by the deep ocean trenches and nutrient-rich upwellings that surround the islands.

The transition from hunting to conservation was not seamless. Many former whaling communities struggled economically during the transition years. Some families lost their primary source of income overnight.

But the *vigias* — those stone watchtowers — provided an unexpected bridge. The same skills that made a good whale spotter — patience, knowledge of currents and weather, an ability to read the surface of the ocean — proved equally valuable for whale-watching tourism. Several former vigias now work as spotters for whale-watching companies, guiding boats to the same animals their fathers once hunted.

"The ocean does not care about our categories," says Sofia Ribeiro, a marine biologist at the University of the Azores. "It does not know the difference between a hunting boat and a watching boat. What matters is that the whales are still here. That is not guaranteed — it is a gift, and it requires our constant attention."

## The Watchtower

On a headland above Lajes do Pico, one of the old *vigias* has been restored as a museum. Inside, photographs of the last hunts hang alongside whale skulls and harpoon tips. A video loop shows grainy footage of a sperm whale surfacing beside an open boat, the harpooner standing in the bow with his weapon raised.

Outside, the view is unchanged from the one the whalers knew. The Atlantic stretches to the horizon in every direction, its surface shifting between blue and grey and silver as clouds pass overhead. Somewhere out there, the whales are moving through water a thousand metres deep, following routes they have travelled for millions of years.

The watchtower stands empty now. But the watching continues.
