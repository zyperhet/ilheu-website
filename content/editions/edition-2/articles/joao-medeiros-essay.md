The Azores sit on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the submarine mountain range where the European and North American tectonic plates pull apart at a rate of about 2.5 centimetres per year. This is not a metaphor, though it works beautifully as one.

To live in the Azores is to live in the middle — not the centre, which implies importance, but the middle, which implies something more unsettling. The middle of the Atlantic. The middle of nowhere and everywhere. Equidistant from Lisbon and New York, from the coast of Africa and the coast of Canada, belonging fully to none of these places and connected, by ocean currents and flight paths and centuries of emigration, to all of them.

My family has lived on Faial for as long as anyone can remember. My great-grandfather hunted whales. My grandfather milked cows. My father taught mathematics at the secondary school in Horta and spent his evenings listening to shortwave radio, pulling in voices from São Paulo, Toronto, Boston — the cities where his brothers and cousins had gone to build lives that the islands could not sustain.
