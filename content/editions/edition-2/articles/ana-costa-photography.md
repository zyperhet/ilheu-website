For ten years, Ana Costa has been photographing the abandoned volcanic observation stations scattered across the Azorean archipelago. Built during the mid-twentieth century, when the science of volcanology was still young and the memory of devastating eruptions was fresh, these small concrete structures were once staffed by men who watched seismographs and measured gas emissions, waiting for the earth to speak.

Most stations were decommissioned in the 1990s, replaced by automated monitoring systems. They stand now as monuments to a particular kind of vigilance — human bodies positioned at the edge of geological uncertainty, listening for tremors that might never come.

Costa's photographs capture these structures in various states of decay: instruments rusted, windows broken, logbooks swollen with damp. But they also capture something harder to name — a quality of attention that lingers in these places, as though the act of watching has left a residue.
