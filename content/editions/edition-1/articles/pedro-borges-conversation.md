The road to Furnas winds through São Miguel's interior, climbing past dairy farms and hydrangea hedgerows before descending into a volcanic valley where steam rises from the earth and the air smells faintly of sulphur. It is here, in one of the Azores' most geologically active areas, that Pedro Borges undertook what he calls "the most humbling project of my career."

Terra Nostra Garden is a 200-year-old botanical park built around a thermal pool fed by iron-rich volcanic springs. For generations, it has been one of the Azores' most beloved landmarks — a place where locals come to swim in warm, rust-coloured water surrounded by centuries-old trees.

When Borges was commissioned to renovate the garden's visitor facilities, he knew the stakes were unusually high.

## Listening to the Landscape

"The first thing I did was nothing," Borges says, standing beside the thermal pool on a morning when mist hangs low between the giant sequoias and tree ferns. "I came here every day for three months. I walked the paths. I sat by the water. I watched how people moved through the space. I listened."

What he heard — and what he ultimately built — reflects an approach to architecture that prizes restraint over spectacle. The new structures at Terra Nostra are almost invisible from certain angles, their low profiles and natural materials dissolving into the botanical landscape that surrounds them.

"In architecture school, we are taught to make statements," he says. "But some places don't need our statements. They need our silence."

## Volcanic Vernacular

Borges's practice, based in Ponta Delgada, specialises in what he calls "volcanic vernacular" — an architectural language that draws on the geological and agricultural traditions of the Azores. His buildings use local basalt, timber from managed Cryptomeria japonica forests, and spatial proportions derived from traditional Azorean farmhouses.

"Every material here tells a geological story," he explains, running his hand along a wall of rough-cut basalt. "This stone was lava once. It flowed down a mountainside, cooled, hardened, and was cut by someone's grandfather. When I use it in a building, I'm not just choosing a material — I'm continuing a conversation that began with an eruption."

The approach has won him recognition beyond the Azores. His work has been featured in Architectural Review, Dezeen, and the Venice Biennale's Portuguese pavilion. But Borges is wary of the attention.

"I don't want to be the architect who makes the Azores fashionable," he says. "I want to be the architect who helps the Azores remain themselves."

## The Challenge of Tourism

Like many in the Azores, Borges is grappling with the rapid growth of tourism on the islands. São Miguel in particular has seen a surge of visitors since low-cost airlines began flying direct from continental Europe and North America.

"Tourism can be wonderful — it brings revenue, it creates jobs, it introduces the world to a place that deserves to be known," he says. "But it can also be destructive. I see proposals for hotels and resorts that could have been designed for any Mediterranean island. They have nothing to do with this place."

His response is not to resist development but to insist on its specificity. Each project, he argues, must emerge from a deep understanding of its particular site — its geology, its climate, its cultural memory.

"Architecture in the Azores must begin with humility," he says. "The landscape was here long before us, and it will outlast anything we build. Our job is not to compete with it but to find our place within it."
