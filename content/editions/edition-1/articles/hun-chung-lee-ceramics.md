Hun-Chung Lee's studio in São Miguel occupies a converted whale-processing station on the island's south coast — a cavernous space of rough stone walls and salt-corroded iron beams that seems purpose-built for an artist who works at the scale of architecture.

The pieces currently in progress are enormous. Three vessels, each over a metre tall, stand in various stages of completion on heavy wooden tables. Their surfaces are rough, almost geological — cratered and ridged in ways that suggest not the potter's wheel but tectonic forces operating over millennia.

"People call them pots," Lee says, with a smile that suggests he has made this correction many times before. "They are not pots. You could not put flowers in them. They are arguments about form."

## Between Continents

Lee was born in Seoul in 1967 and trained at the Korean National University of Arts before moving to Europe in his thirties. He spent a decade in London, where his work caught the attention of collectors and institutions. His pieces now sit in the collections of the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Korean National Museum of Contemporary Art, and — famously — the private collections of Brad Pitt and Norman Foster.

But it was a chance visit to the Azores in 2019 that redirected his practice entirely.

"I came for a holiday," he says. "I stayed because I recognised something. The volcanic landscape — the way lava cools and cracks, the way basalt columns form, the way hot springs deposit mineral layers over centuries — these are ceramic processes. The earth here is doing what I do in my kiln, but at a scale I can only dream of."

## The Nine Islands Project

Lee is now two years into what he calls his most ambitious undertaking: a series of site-specific ceramic installations across all nine islands of the Azorean archipelago. Each work will respond to the particular geology and culture of its island.

On São Miguel, the focus is thermal — works inspired by the fumaroles and hot springs of Furnas. On Faial, devastated by a volcanic eruption in 1957, the work will address destruction and renewal. On Corvo, the smallest and most remote island, Lee plans a single, monumental vessel placed on the rim of the extinct caldera that forms the island's centre.

"Each island is a different geological personality," he explains. "São Miguel is lush and green and steaming. Pico is dominated by the mountain. Flores is waterfalls and cliffs. The ceramics must respond to these differences — not illustrate them, but embody them."

The project has the backing of the Regional Government of the Azores and several international arts foundations. Lee estimates it will take another three to four years to complete.

## The Kiln and the Volcano

In his studio, Lee fires his largest pieces in a wood-burning kiln he built himself, using techniques adapted from Korean *onggi* traditions. The firing process takes several days and produces unpredictable results — surfaces scarred by flame, glazes that bubble and crack, colours that shift from black to amber depending on where in the kiln the piece sat.

"Control is an illusion," Lee says, watching as an assistant loads wood into the kiln's firebox. "The best ceramics are collaborations between the potter and the fire. I set the conditions. The fire makes the decisions."

It is, he suggests, not unlike the relationship between the Azoreans and their volcanic islands — a negotiation between human intention and geological force, in which the earth always has the final word.

"The Azores are made of fire and water," he says. "Ceramics are born from the same elements. There is a deep kinship here. I feel it every time I open the kiln."
